## 2 · What the review found

### 2.1 The Critical, and why it is the milestone's defining lesson

`MERGE` and `SALVAGE` destroyed items through `Inventory.Remove` and never cleared the loadout slot. A slot **names** an item rather than copying one, so destroying worn gear left the aggregate in the state `Player.RequireLoadoutResolves` refuses — and it refuses by **throwing**, because reaching it is a handler defect rather than an illegal player action. Salvaging a piece of gear the hero was wearing threw `InvalidOperationException` out of `Apply` on a perfectly legal command.

`Player.DiscardItem` exists for exactly this and does both halves together. **It had zero production callers.**

Three of the four review dimensions found it independently. What makes it a milestone-review catch rather than a per-task one is the shape of how it was missed:

1. M4-10 authored the invariant and **wrote the obligation down**, in `GapRegister`, naming **M4-04** as owner, in as many words: *"ANY OPERATION THAT DESTROYS AN ITEM MUST TAKE IT OFF THE HERO IN THE SAME CHANGE."*
2. M4-04 shipped merge, salvage and enhance **without it**.
3. Nothing went red, because `EQUIP` was `Deferred` for the whole of M4 — no command could fill a slot, so no fixture could reach the state.
4. **M7-00d wired `EQUIP`** and made it live, in a different milestone, without either task seeing the other.

So: a correctly-identified obligation, with a named owner, recorded in the register built for that purpose — and it still did not survive its owner shipping. **The register records; it does not enforce.**

### 2.2 The same shape, twice more

- The **tunable-marker baseline**'s expiry test asserts a `closedBy` task id *exists* in the tracker. It never asserts the task is still **open**. Eight entries name owners that have already shipped — two of them named M4-04, which merged *inside the milestone that was supposed to close them*. That is S4's failure mode occurring inside the mechanism built to prevent S4 failures.
- **`AutoSalvageFilter`** (75 lines + 149 lines of tests) and the entire `ItemAvailability.LOCKED` path — `InventoryAccess`'s refusal arm, `Enhance`'s deliberate lock-passthrough, `Equip`'s "a locked item is equippable" ruling — were authored, tested, and unreachable, because no command wrote them. **Fixed by the retro's own ruling** (§4).

Three instances in one milestone of *declared, owned, and quietly not done*.

### 2.3 Naming that contradicts arithmetic

Two tuning keys fed the same ordinal predicate (`HardPity.Fires`, contract `misses >= everyNth - 1`) under names meaning the opposite: `consecutiveMissesBeforeForce: 6` and `guaranteeAfterConsecutiveMisses: 4`. Now `forceOnNthKill` / `guaranteeOnNthPick`.

Worth recording precisely: **the code was right and a reviewer proposed breaking it.** The code-quality pass recommended adding `+1` to the chest-pick guarantee. Reading `24` §4.3 D1 (*"On the **6th**, force"*) and §4.9 directly showed both keys already implemented the document correctly — the defect was the name. Applying that recommendation would have shifted a live fairness guarantee by a draw. The milestone also contains its own counterexample: `DraftGuarantees` passes `+1` for its two *count*-named keys and none for its ordinal-named one, and gets all three right.

### 2.4 The milestone goal, versus the sum of its tasks

M4's goal is *"the between-run loop that a run **actually feeds**"*. Every task met its own description. The **join** between them is owned by no row:

`GearStatDerivation`, `SetBonusResolver`, `InventorySorting`, `UnlockGate` and `InventoryComparison` all have **zero production callers**. `Run.StartingLoadout` is written at `START_RUN` and read by nothing but its own snapshot. `GearEnhancement.StatMultiplier` is uncalled, so a `+15` item derives the same stats as a `+0` and the POWER sort ranks a 713-stone investment below a lucky fresh roll.

M4 delivered the run→loop direction whole and none of the loop→run direction. The exit criterion does not ask for it — it asks that the loadout be *carried*, and it is — so this is not a failed clause. It is the gap between a **criterion** and a **goal**. Filed as **M4-16**, to be ruled with **M7-06b**, the identical hole seen from the client.

## 3 · Process — including my own defects

### 3.1 I violated S10, and it nearly shipped a broken guarantee

I dispatched two fix agents against **one checkout** with only a file-territory split. Their S1 mutation probes leaked across the split twice, including `ChestPickGuarantee.GuaranteeFires => true` — a gold-tier chest on **every** pick — announced to the other agent as an intentional change it should not revert.

The second agent caught it, reverted both, and **stopped rather than report a green result it could not trust**. That was the right call under a bad instruction from me.

The recovery matters as much as the cause: a green suite **cannot** detect a probe reverted and re-applied between test runs. Only reading the diff can. Both lines were re-verified directly, the entire production diff was read line by line, and a BOM/line-ending audit was run over every changed file.

### 3.2 I wrote the retro into the wrong checkout, and then misdiagnosed it

This retro, the steering updates and the skill edits were written to `C:/Git/slay-idle-repeat/.claude/…` — **the main repository's working tree**, not this worktree's. The worktree lives *inside* `.claude/worktrees/`, so the two paths differ only by a segment in the middle, and both exist. Every write succeeded. Nothing failed loudly.

I noticed only because `git ls-files` did not list `M4.md` after I believed I had committed it. **And my first diagnosis was wrong**: seeing tracked files apparently reverted, I concluded an agent had restored them from `HEAD` and wrote that into this document as an S1 finding. It was not true. `git status` in the main repo showed all three sitting there, modified and untracked, exactly where I had put them. The files were moved into the worktree and the main checkout restored.

Two things worth keeping from it, and the second matters more than the first:

- **A worktree nested under a path you also edit by absolute path is a trap.** Prefer paths relative to the working directory, or verify with `git status` in the tree you *think* you edited — a successful write is not evidence you wrote to the right tree.
- 🔴 **I reached for the dramatic explanation before the boring one.** "A concurrent agent destroyed my work" fitted a real pattern this milestone had already produced twice (§3.1), which is exactly what made it plausible enough to write down without checking. The boring explanation — I typed the wrong path — was one `git status` away. This is S21's shape turned on myself: *before ruling against an apparent gap, check what the repo has already decided about it.* An incident narrative is a claim about repo state, and mine are as unreliable as any quoted number.

The related discipline still stands on its own merits: **commit conductor artefacts as they are written.** Had these been committed immediately, the wrong-tree write would have failed at `git add` instead of surviving to be misread an hour later.

### 3.3 The kickoff was 100 % auto-accepted, and one decision was unimplementable

All 14 decisions were the conductor's own recommendations taken as answers. One — R5's pre-assignment of `SnapshotSchema.SchemaVersion` per task — was **mechanically impossible**: `SnapshotFieldOrderPinTests` demands a pin section for every intervening version, so a task jumping to a reserved higher number would have to invent three other tasks' serialisation layouts. Caught mid-run by the first agent that tried to use it (§A9). `GapRegister` already said so *before* the kickoff was written; the kickoff did not check. S21's exact shape, committed by the conductor.

The salvageable half is real: making each lane **aware** a shared counter exists worked perfectly. Every agent reported its number prominently and unprompted, and no collision went silent across five lanes.

### 3.4 Lane planning was revised mid-run, correctly

The kickoff made M4-04 (Forge) and M4-05 (Inventory) siblings. M4-03's own report showed that was wrong — every forge operation presupposes the inventory M4-05 builds. Caught at the first merge and re-planned (§A4). Producer→consumer (S11) was applied to *types* at kickoff (§R4) and got right; applied to *storage*, it was missed.

### 3.5 Citations by line number

`12 §66`, `14 §662` and `09 §53` are **line numbers written as section references**. `12` stops at §9.1, `14` at §16.6, `09` at §8. They propagated to 20 sites across production doc comments, test failure messages, an architecture rule's licence string, and a design document. Nothing checks that a `§` reference resolves.

🔴 **The measured consequence, reported because it was measured rather than assumed.** M1 carry-forward 12 makes inventory size a per-command cost — `InMemoryGame` clones the player on every command. Release, 720 commands, four runs:

| range | 13.9–21.1 ms | **117.6–167.4 ms** | 7.27–12.04 |

## 5 · What to keep doing

- **Give the exit criterion its own task, owned by no one else, running last.** M4-15 justified itself on day one.
- **`LuckRoutingRuleTests` is the reference pattern** for architecture rules here: multiple arms, closed surfaces in both directions, identity floors rather than count floors, stated over façade *members* because enum tokens fold to `ldc.i4`, and its own blind spots enumerated in the file.
- **Report the mutation that stayed green.** Several agents did this unprompted (`ForceRarityAtLeast → BelowRarity` being non-discriminating; `if (false)` failing to compile under warnings-as-errors; three guards found subsumed during the retro's implementation, one of which was deleted rather than kept as decoration). A probe that passes is a finding.
- **Correct the conductor.** Agents corrected my figures and framings repeatedly and were right nearly every time. S9's corollary — *your claims about repo state are as unreliable as a number quoted from a report* — earned its place again.
- **Stop and report rather than ship a green you do not trust.** The fix agent that halted mid-pass on detecting cross-contamination did the right thing.
